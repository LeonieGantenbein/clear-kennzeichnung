# Culture before Labelling

A good system alone is not enough. Whether a label is put into practice, voluntary or mandatory, depends on how we want to deal with AI involvement. That is something we have yet to agree on.

## The problem: transparency is penalised

Anyone who discloses today that an AI was involved takes a risk. The text is quickly devalued, no matter how much of one’s own work it contains. As long as openness is penalised, only those who can afford it will label, and for everyone else it remains reasonable to say nothing.

Perhaps this stems from a deeper inhibition: we measure achievement by the time someone has spent. Anyone who uses a tool is suspected of having reached the goal faster and has to justify themselves, as if what counted were not the result but the time spent on it.

Yet the suspicion is often wrong. A good text that has been improved with AI comes about in a long back-and-forth in which we correct and improve again and again. The human share in this loop, the human in the loop, is correspondingly high. Challenging a text with an AI again and again can take more time and effort than writing it without one.

This creates a strange situation: using AI is expected. But no one is supposed to notice. It is precisely this that strengthens shadow use, with all the unease it brings as a side effect.

## Who writes, who reads

There is unease on both sides, but it does not concern the same thing.

Those who write hesitate above all when the AI has done more than put in commas. If it has rearranged the structure or sharpened a thought, it becomes unclear how much of the text is still one’s own. It is now possible to say in the prompt that the AI should change the text in tracked-changes mode. This lets us follow every change and accept or reject it, and it remains transparent what the AI proposes to change in the text. Even so, there is only one word for all of this today: “AI-generated”. Anyone who wants to be honest would therefore have to attach a label that claims far more than actually happened. So one would rather say nothing.

Those who read ask a different question. Whether a sentence comes from a human or from a machine is not the real concern. What is unsettling is something else: that no one read the text before it was published. And that evidence might be invented or interpretations slipped in without the reader noticing.

This shows what matters in labelling. R says whether a human has checked the text and stands behind it. E says how far the sources can be trusted. Responsibility for a statement always remains with whoever makes it, not with the AI.

## What needs to change culturally

1. **Disclosure must not devalue.** An honest statement is a sign of care. It is not a confession. What matters is what someone stands behind, not whether a tool was involved.
2. **Differentiate instead of labelling wholesale.** Proofreading is a fundamentally different intervention from a text generated by an AI. Calling both “AI” without distinction makes honesty expensive.
3. **Put responsibility at the centre.** The shared norm should not be “without AI” but “checked and taken responsibility for” (R1). Whoever publishes bears the responsibility anyway. R shows whether it was actually taken up.
4. **Institutions lead the way.** Schools, universities, editorial offices and publishers can use the same five statements to set out what they expect (see chapter [“Specification”](SPECIFICATION.md), section [“Reversal”](SPECIFICATION.md#reversal-clear-as-an-expectation-profile)). Those who name levels instead of banning tools make disclosure routine.
5. **Lead by example.** Labelling becomes normal when those who can afford it make a start. In doing so, they lower the risk for everyone else. That is why I label my own texts.

## When labelling becomes effective

A label proves nothing. It remains a self-declaration, and one can lie with it. Its value lies elsewhere: it gives us a language in which we can draw distinctions at all. A text that conceals how it came about pretends to be something it is not.

CLEAR only becomes effective once the absence of a label also says something. Then a culture has emerged in which people ask why a text is not labelled.
