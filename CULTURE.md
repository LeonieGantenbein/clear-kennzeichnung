# Culture before Labelling

A theory of CLEAR labelling alone is not enough. It presupposes a cultural negotiation that has not yet taken place. Without this culture, no label – whether voluntary or mandatory – will actually be put into practice.

## The problem: transparency is penalised today

At present, disclosure is a risk. Anyone who discloses must expect the text to be devalued, regardless of how much of their own work it contains. So people stay silent. As long as transparency is penalised, only those who can afford it will label – and opacity remains the rational choice.

There is also a deeper inhibition: our idea of work performance is closely tied to time. Anyone who is faster with a tool almost has to justify themselves, as if what were rewarded were not the result but the time spent. Using AI is in demand – but woe to those in whom it shows.

## Two different kinds of unease

As authors, we often feel uneasy about conceptual or linguistic revision by AI, because that is where our own contribution becomes contestable. Today, both fall under the same blanket label, “AI-generated”. This is precisely what gives rise to the diffuse unease that prevents honest disclosure.

As readers, our unease concerns a different question: not who formulated the sentences, but whether anyone stands behind them. The real suspicion is not that a machine wrote the text, but that no one read it before publication. That is why R is the decisive statement. And E affects us directly, because invented evidence and slipped-in interpretations are harms that even attentive reading cannot always catch.

## What needs to change culturally

1. **Disclosure must not devalue.** An honest statement must count as a sign of care, not as a confession. The measure is what someone stands behind – not whether a tool was involved.
2. **Differentiate instead of labelling wholesale.** Proofreading, substantive editing and generated content are different things. A culture that calls everything “AI” makes honesty expensive.
3. **Put responsibility at the centre.** The shared norm should not be “without AI” but “checked and taken responsibility for” (R1).
4. **Institutions lead the way.** Schools, universities, editorial offices and publishers can use the same five statements to say what they expect (see chapters [“Specification”](SPECIFICATION.md), [“Reversal”](SPECIFICATION.md#reversal-clear-as-an-expectation-profile)). Those who name levels instead of banning tools make disclosure routine.
5. **Lead by example.** Labelling becomes normal when people who can afford it start doing it – and thereby lower the risk for everyone else.

## When labelling becomes effective

The label proves nothing; it remains a self-declaration. Its value lies in providing a language in which distinctions can be made at all. A text that conceals how it came about pretends to be something it is not.

CLEAR only becomes effective once its absence is meaningful again – that is, once a culture has emerged in which people ask why a text is not labelled.
