# Culture before Labelling

A system of labelling alone is not enough. Whether a label – voluntary or mandatory – is actually put into practice depends on a cultural understanding that has yet to be reached.

## The problem: transparency is penalised

At present, disclosure is a risk. Anyone who discloses must expect the text to be devalued, regardless of how much of their own work it contains. The obvious consequence is silence. As long as transparency is sanctioned, only those who can afford it will label, and opacity remains the reasonable choice.

There is also a deeper inhibition: our understanding of performance is closely tied to the time invested. Anyone who reaches a result faster with a tool comes under pressure to justify themselves, as if what were rewarded were not the result but the time spent. The use of AI is expected – yet it is not supposed to show.

## Two forms of unease

Authors often feel uneasy about conceptual or linguistic revision by AI, because this is precisely where their own contribution becomes contestable. Today, both fall under the same blanket label, “AI-generated”. This lack of differentiation gives rise to the diffuse unease that prevents honest disclosure.

The unease of readers concerns a different question: not who formulated the sentences, but whether anyone stands behind them. The real suspicion is not that a machine wrote the text, but that no one read it before publication. That is why R is the decisive statement. The statement E affects readers directly, because invented evidence and slipped-in interpretations cause harm that even attentive reading cannot always prevent.

## What needs to change culturally

1. **Disclosure must not devalue.** An honest statement should be understood as an expression of care, not as a confession. What matters is what someone stands behind – not whether a tool was involved.
2. **Differentiate instead of labelling wholesale.** Proofreading, substantive editing and generated content are fundamentally different interventions. A culture that calls all of this “AI” without distinction makes honesty expensive.
3. **Put responsibility at the centre.** The shared norm should not be “without AI” but “checked and taken responsibility for” (R1).
4. **Institutions lead the way.** Schools, universities, editorial offices and publishers can use the same five statements to formulate what they expect (see chapter [“Specification”](SPECIFICATION.md), section [“Reversal”](SPECIFICATION.md#reversal-clear-as-an-expectation-profile)). Those who name levels instead of banning tools make disclosure routine.
5. **Lead by example.** Labelling becomes normal when those who can afford it begin, thereby lowering the risk for everyone else.

## When labelling becomes effective

A label proves nothing; it remains a self-declaration. Its value lies in providing a language in which distinctions can be drawn at all. A text that conceals how it came about pretends to be something it is not.

CLEAR only becomes effective once the absence of a label says something again – that is, once a culture has emerged in which people ask why a text is not labelled.
