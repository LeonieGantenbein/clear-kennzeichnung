# Specification v1.0

## Starting point

Technical watermarks will not create transparency for texts. At best, a watermark answers whether an AI model was involved, but not in what way. It says nothing about the substance of the involvement, its extent or the question of who stands behind the text. If a human wrote the text and an AI merely smoothed it, it does not apply at all. Nor does the EU’s AI Act require differentiated information; it requires labelling only in a few narrowly defined cases.

Where neither regulation nor technology helps, voluntary disclosure remains. CLEAR structures it in five statements per person involved.

## The five axes

### C – Conception

Axis C records who developed the line of thought of a text.

- **C0** – The line of thought was developed exclusively by humans.
- **C1** – The AI served as a discussion partner during conception.
- **C2** – Individual content parts were worked out by the AI.
- **C3** – The content was comprehensively generated by the AI.

### L – Language (linguistic editing)

Axis L records how far the AI intervened in the linguistic form of a text.

- **L0** – The language was formulated without AI.
- **L1** – Proofreading: the AI corrected spelling, grammar and punctuation.
- **L2** – Stylistic editing: the AI improved the wording without changing the content.
- **L3** – Substantive editing: the AI intervened in structure and argument.
- **L4** – Drafting: the AI wrote out the text from content that the human provided or worked out with it.

Suffix: **(t)** for a text translated by the AI.

Translation is not a level of its own but an exception indicated by the suffix. It already counts as proofreading: a pure translation by the AI is L(t)1. If the AI also edited or drafted the text, before or after translating it, the corresponding level is given, for example L(t)3 or L(t)4. This keeps translations to a single number per axis as well.

### E – Evidence

Axis E concerns the sources on which a text rests. Its levels indicate how far the AI stood between the human and the material.

- **E0** – Access to the sources and their processing took place without AI.
- **E1** – Source access: the AI found, obtained or translated sources; the human read and interpreted them.
- **E2** – Source processing: the AI read and summarised sources, and work continued with these summaries instead of the primary sources. Whatever is missing there is also missing in the text.
- **E3** – Source interpretation: the AI interpreted what the sources say, what is sound and where they contradict each other; this interpretation was adopted.
- **E4** – Interpretation of results: the AI drew a conclusion from the results that appears in the text as a result.

Suffix: **(p)** for published, **(n)** for own, unpublished material.

The suffix indicates whether readers can verify what happened: with (p), the source is accessible; with (n), it is not. An interpretation at level E(p)4 can sooner or later be checked. E(n)4, by contrast – an interpretation of one’s own data proposed by the AI – escapes any external scrutiny. That is precisely why it must be disclosed.

### A – Attribution

Axis A names the AI models used and the period of their use. Anyone reading the text years later can thus place it in relation to the state of the tools with which it was produced.

### R – Responsibility

Axis R makes visible whether a human has checked the text. Responsibility for a statement always remains with whoever makes it, not with the AI.

- **R1** – The text was fully checked and is taken responsibility for by the human.
- **R2** – The text was spot-checked and is therefore not fully taken responsibility for.
- **R3** – The text was published unchecked, with the risk of having to take responsibility for it nonetheless.

## Notation

```
CLEAR (role): C?, L?, E(p|n)?, A: models, period, R?
```

- The highest level reached is given for each axis; it includes the levels below.
- Each person involved declares their own share and takes responsibility for it. Several people involved – such as author, editor and translator – each get a line of their own.
- The label appears before or after the text.

## Example

```
CLEAR (author): C1, L3, E(p)1, A: Claude Opus 4.8, Fable 5, Opus 5, 04–09/2026, R1
```

Reading: The line of thought is the author’s own. The AI served as a sparring partner – it contradicted, asked questions and raised objections, but did not dictate the position (C1). In the writing, its involvement went beyond smoothing and concerned the structure, the arrangement of the arguments and the transitions (L3). The AI researched references; all sources are published and verifiable (E(p)1). Every passage was checked and is taken responsibility for (R1). Several models are named because the text was written over several months.

Further examples from study, research, work, public life and private life, with several contributors, translations and all three levels of R, can be found in the chapter [“Examples”](EXAMPLES.md).

## Guidance on classification

The boundary between L2 and L3 runs between wording and argument. Anyone who reads a revised text as a whole sees only the result and can no longer distinguish what came from them. The impression that it was merely smoothed therefore leads systematically to too low a classification.

It is advisable always to have linguistic revisions by AI made in tracked-changes mode (Word: “Track Changes”; Google Docs: Suggesting mode; plain text: comparing versions) and to review the suggestions one by one. This instruction can be included directly in the prompt.

- If there are changes that merge sentences, rearrange the order or tighten an argument, it is L3 – regardless of how many purely stylistic corrections stand alongside them. The axis measures the deepest involvement, not the most frequent.
- Anyone who has confirmed every change individually has actually checked the text; R1 thus remains achievable.

## Reversal: CLEAR as an expectation profile

CLEAR is first of all a self-declaration, but it can also be reversed: those who receive texts can use the same statements to set out what they expect. Schools, upper secondary schools and universities would thus gain a language they have so far lacked. An example for final theses:

| Axis | Requirement | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| R | R1 mandatory | A signature, as it were; the only level compatible with an examination performance |
| L | up to L2, in writing-intensive subjects possibly up to L3 | depending on the subject |
| C | at most C1 | The line of thought is precisely the achievement being assessed |
| E | E1 | Source criticism is the craft a thesis must demonstrate |

An institution that sets this down does not ban tools, but names levels and teaches how to work with them.

## Other media (open)

CLEAR is designed for texts. Images, moving images and code require an adapted form; the basic question remains the same: in what respects was the AI involved, and who stands behind it?

- A and R can be adopted unchanged.
- Image and video: design takes the place of language (retouching, additions, generation of entire image parts). For documentary material, E becomes the question of whether what is shown actually took place.
- Code: implementation takes the place of language. E asks about adopted libraries and code bases, R about tests and review.

How the levels should be worked out in detail is best determined by those who work with these media. Suggestions are welcome.

## Limits

CLEAR has not yet been tested or evaluated in practice and is certainly incomplete. The label proves nothing; it remains a self-declaration. A precondition for its effectiveness is a culture in which disclosure is not penalised (→ chapter [“Culture before Labelling”](CULTURE.md)).
